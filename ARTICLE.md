# The Interval That Lied Politely: What Happens When Conformal Prediction Meets a Resource That Keeps Growing

A capacity forecast that says "14 days" is a lie of omission. Fourteen days assuming what noise level, what growth regime, what confidence. Every data engineer who has been paged by a full tablespace at 3 a.m. has also, at some point, watched a capacity dashboard confidently predict weeks of runway the night before it ran out. The number was not wrong because the model was bad. It was wrong because nobody had asked, or answered, how wrong the number was allowed to be.

This project started from that irritation and a specific, falsifiable question: can a forecasting pipeline for infrastructure capacity produce an interval that is actually calibrated, meaning a 90% interval that truly contains the truth about 90% of the time, using a technique with a real mathematical guarantee behind it rather than a rule of thumb? The technique exists. It is called conformal prediction, and the flavor used here, Conformalized Quantile Regression (CQR), comes from a 2019 NeurIPS paper by Romano, Patterson and Candes. The premise of CQR is almost suspiciously simple: take any quantile regression model, however crude, hold out a calibration set it never trained on, measure how wrong its intervals were on that calibration set, and use that error to inflate future intervals by exactly the right amount to hit a target coverage level, no matter what the underlying data distribution looks like.

That last clause, no matter what the underlying data distribution looks like, is the part worth being skeptical of. It sounds like a free lunch, and the fine print is that it depends on an assumption called exchangeability, roughly, that the calibration data and the future data being predicted come from interchangeable draws of the same distribution. For a lot of machine learning problems that assumption is a reasonable idealization. For a monotonically growing resource, it is not, and the interesting part of this project is finding out exactly how it fails and by how much, instead of citing the guarantee and moving on.

## Building something to break

The plan was to simulate a small fleet of infrastructure resources, tablespaces, queues, disk volumes, with three distinct growth personalities: a boring linear grower, a seasonal one with weekday batch jobs baked in, and a bursty one that sits flat for weeks and then jumps, the way a resource behaves right after someone ships a backfill job or changes a retention policy. Thirty synthetic series, each with a known, noise-free ground truth trajectory hidden underneath the noisy observations, so that every forecast could eventually be graded against a fact rather than another guess.

On top of that fleet sits a global quantile regression model, three gradient-boosted regressors trained with pinball loss at the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles, pooled across every series so the model has enough data to learn something about level, trend, and volatility instead of overfitting to one time series in isolation. Then comes the calibration step: hold out a slice of each series in time order, measure the model's raw error on it, and use CQR's formula to inflate the naive interval into a calibrated one.

The first backtest looked encouraging. On the held-out test window, the calibrated model reached 85.2% empirical coverage against a 90% target, versus 82.1% for the uncalibrated baseline. An improvement, if a modest one. The instinct at that point is to write it up as a clean win and move on. That instinct is exactly the one worth resisting, because an aggregate number over a mixed population can hide a number that is very good and a number that is very bad, averaged into something merely mediocre.

## The finding that mattered more than the headline number

Splitting the test set by one simple condition, whether the resource's current utilization level was higher than anything the model had ever seen during training, exposed the real story. On the 89.3% of test points still inside the training range, coverage was 89.1%, essentially exactly on target. On the remaining 10.7%, coverage collapsed to 52.6%, barely better than a coin flip for a 90% interval, and the direction of the bias flipped and grew roughly tenfold, systematically under-forecasting exactly the cases where a resource had broken into new territory.

The mechanism is not subtle once you look for it. A gradient-boosted tree is, at its core, a lookup table: every leaf outputs a constant learned from the training examples that landed in it. Ask it to predict for an input outside the range it was trained on, and it cannot extrapolate; it just returns whatever constant its nearest leaf happens to hold, anchored to the last levels it actually saw. For a resource that is, by the very nature of a capacity problem, always growing toward levels it has not reached yet, this is not an edge case. It is the central case. The forecast that matters most, the one for the resource about to break new ground, is exactly the one this architecture is worst at.

Conformal calibration does not fix this, and it is worth being precise about why. CQR's guarantee is a marginal one, averaged over the whole population the calibration set represents. It has nothing to say about a specific slice of the input space where the exchangeability assumption itself has quietly broken down, because the calibration set, drawn from the pre-shift distribution, never saw the shifted region either. The interval widens uniformly, correcting for the model's average error, while remaining blind to the fact that its error is not uniform at all.

## A second, smaller lie to catch

The project has a second piece worth mentioning briefly: once you have a calibrated numeric forecast, someone eventually asks an LLM to turn it into a sentence a human will actually read in a Slack message. That convenience opens its own failure mode, an LLM confidently stating a number that was never computed. The fix here is intentionally unglamorous: a regex-based fact-checker that extracts every number in a generated narrative and verifies it against the numbers that were actually produced by the forecasting pipeline, flagging anything that does not match within tolerance. Tested against 24 narratives from an independent LLM instance, half seeded with a deliberately fabricated figure, the checker reached perfect precision and recall. That result comes with its own honest caveat: the fabrications tested were large, fifteen to forty percent off. A hallucination that lands inside the tolerance window would slip through, because this is a threshold check, not a semantic one, and pretending otherwise would undercut the entire point of building a project about honest evaluation.

## What this is actually worth

None of this is an argument against conformal prediction, which did exactly what its theory promises inside its assumptions. It is an argument for measuring where the assumptions end, instead of quoting a coverage number as though it applies uniformly everywhere the model will ever be asked a question. For anyone building capacity forecasting on top of tree-based models, the practical takeaway is concrete: track whether an input has moved past the training distribution's range, and treat that condition as a trigger for a wider, more conservative fallback interval rather than trusting the calibrated number as-is. That is a five-line monitoring check that would have caught the exact failure mode measured here, and it did not make it into this version of the project on purpose, because diagnosing the gap honestly, with real numbers, was the more valuable thing to ship first.
